A while back, one of my study mantras was simple: **claim the orphaned pots.** When preflop action goes limp-limp-check and nobody's shown strength, be the one who takes it down on the flop.

It's easy to write that down. It's harder to know if you're actually doing it — or where. So I ran my own database: every hand where I saw a flop, won the pot, and it never went to showdown. No cherry-picking, no bad-beat stories. Just the filter and the results.

> 54 hands. ~920 BB won. Zero of them lost.

That part isn't surprising — if you win without a showdown, by definition nobody beat you to the pot. The interesting part was *where* these wins came from.

## The pool defense stat I didn't expect

I assumed most of these would come from late position — button steals, cutoff isolations, the classic "everyone's weak, I'm last to act, I take it" spots. Position charts tend to reinforce that instinct.

The data disagreed.

> 63% of these pots (34 of 54) came from the blinds — 16 from the big blind, 18 from the small blind — versus only 37% combined from button, cutoff, and middle position.

In other words: I wasn't mostly *stealing* orphaned pots from late position. I was mostly *defending* them from the blinds — seeing a cheap flop after a multiway limp, and being the one willing to bet when everyone else checked it down.

That's a genuinely different skill than the one the mantra described, and worth knowing the difference. It's also worth noting the two skills don't pay the same: by hand count, the blinds dominate, but the average button pot ran bigger in BB terms than the average blind pot — late-position steals are rarer here, but when they land, they land larger.

## Three hands that show the pattern

**1. The cheap small-blind steal.**
Multiway limped pot, six players see a flop of 4♣-2♣-3♣. Action checks to me in the small blind, and I fire a small bet — nobody had anything to defend with, and the pot was mine before the turn even came out. Total pot: 4 BB. Not exciting on its own, but this is the purest version of the pattern: nobody wanted it, so I took it.

**2. The checked-around big blind take.**
Five-way limped pot, flop of Q♦-9♠-7♦ checks all the way around. The turn brings a 6♦, and instead of checking a third time, I lead out. Everyone folds. This is the mantra working exactly as intended — the field showed zero interest on the flop, and I was the one who converted that into a pot on the turn instead of letting it die at showdown.

**3. The bigger multi-street pot.**
Not every orphaned-pot win is a small, single bet. In this hand, I called a small preflop raise from the big blind, then led out on a 8♥-7♠-7♣ flop, barreled the turn (4♠) when called, and fired again on the river (J♣) when my opponent kept calling instead of showing strength. Three streets of pressure, and they folded the river rather than pay off. This is the same underlying pattern — nobody had shown real strength — just played out over more streets and a bigger pot (52 BB).

## What I'm taking from this

The mantra wasn't wrong, but it was incomplete. "Claim the orphaned pots" is really two different skills:

- **Late-position stealing** — betting into a weak field when I'm last to act.
- **Blind defense conversion** — seeing a cheap flop from the blinds and being willing to bet a checked-around pot instead of giving up.

The second one is doing more of the actual work right now. That changes what I should be drilling in study sessions — less "how do I steal from the button," more "how do I recognize a checked-around pot from the blinds and take it."

Next up: I'm running the same check against my river bluff-catcher decisions, using the "can I name 3 bluffs" rule from my study notes. If the blinds data taught me anything, it's that the instinct and the data don't always agree — and the data wins.
